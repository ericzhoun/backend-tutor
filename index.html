Self-study syllabus · Fifteen weeks

Backend System Design

From cost math to field judgment: the 23 canonical system-design concepts, 35 real architectures, and a field manual of incident command, code review, and the senior decision track — distilled from a 17-volume field document library.

6 parts 11 modules 23 concepts 35 case studies 17 field documents ~9 h / week
01

How this course works

Every week runs the same four-step rhythm. The order matters: you diagram before you build, and you write a design doc after you build — because the doc is where the trade-offs stop being hand-wavy.

StepTimeWhat you do
Read3 hStudy the module's concepts from the core reading libraries.
Diagram2 hRedraw the architecture from memory — no peeking. If you can't draw it, you can't build it.
Build3 hImplement the module's lab: small, working, real. A queue that "mostly works" teaches you exactly-once lies.
Design doc1 hOne page: requirements, estimates, architecture, top two bottlenecks. The prompt is given per module.

Two standing rules. Case studies come after concepts, never before — they are the exam, not the lecture. And every module ends by answering the same question in writing: what breaks at 100× scale?

From Week 13 the rhythm is unchanged, but the material switches from textbooks to the field document library (section 07): labs become drills, reviews, and written records — the same skills, tested where they were learned.

Module syllabus

Part I — Foundations · Weeks 1–2

Know what you're up against

Before architecture: the cost math of scale, then everything between a user's tap and your server.

M01

Scaling Fundamentals

Week 1 · 2 concepts · drill-heavy

Goal. Reason about load mathematically before touching any architecture diagram. Most design failures are arithmetic failures wearing an architecture costume.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Horizontal vs Vertical Scalingwhen each is cheaper, their failure and cost curves, and why "just add machines" hides state problems rather than solving them
Back-of-the-Envelope EstimationQPS, storage, and bandwidth math from first principles; the latency numbers every engineer should know cold

DrillMemorize the anchor numbers: 86,400 s/day; RTT ~1 ms same-region, 50–150 ms cross-region; SSD sequential ~100 MB/s and random reads 50–100 µs; a single Postgres primary handles ~10–50K writes/s; a single Redis ~100K ops/s. Practice the capacity chain aloud: DAU → QPS → storage → bandwidth — "Assuming X, and assuming Y, that puts us around Z." Plan for peak, not average: single-region consumer 2.5–3×, global 1.5–2×, B2B business-hours 4–5×, flash-sale 10×+.

LabEstimate QPS, storage growth per day, bandwidth, and cache footprint for five products: URL shortener, news feed, group chat, video streaming, ride hailing. Worked reference: 1B URLs/day ≈ 11.6K writes/s; at a 100:1 read ratio, ≈ 1.16M reads/s.

Field readingSystem Design Reality (the 1K → 10M phase narrative) · Interviews Vol IV ch. 02 (capacity without guessing).

Design doc"Estimate the infrastructure for a WhatsApp-class messenger at 50M DAU."

M02

The Request Path

Week 2 · 4 concepts

Goal. Master everything between a user's tap and your server — and know exactly where each concern lives: edge, gateway, or service.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Load BalancingL4 vs L7, algorithms (round robin, least connections, consistent hashing), health checks, and removing the balancer itself as a single point of failure
CDNedge caching, pull vs push, cache-control headers, and what a CDN cannot help with (dynamic, personalized, low-fan-out content)
API Gatewayrouting, auth termination, aggregation — and when the gateway becomes a bottleneck or a monolith-in-waiting
Rate Limitingtoken bucket vs sliding window, distributed limiters, and the fail-open vs fail-closed decision

LabImplement a token-bucket rate limiter; then draw the complete request path for a read-heavy product, edge to database.

Design doc"Launch-day traffic is 40× forecast. Where does the path break first, and in what order?"

Part II — The Data Layer · Weeks 3–5

State is where systems get hard

Scale storage deliberately. Choose consistency like a contract, not an accident.

M03

Storage at Scale

Weeks 3–4 · 4 concepts · build week

Goal. Make databases scale on purpose. Every sharding war story starts with a shard key chosen casually on a Friday.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Database Scalingindexes, denormalization, read replicas, and SQL vs NoSQL selection by access pattern — not fashion
Replicationleader–follower, multi-leader, and leaderless topologies; sync vs async trade-offs; failover and split brain
Shardingshard-key choice, hotspots, the real cost of resharding at 2 a.m., and consistent hashing as the fix
Partitioningrange vs hash partitioning, skew, and rebalancing strategies

LabBuild a key-value store with consistent-hashing sharding (routed through Build Your Own X). It must survive adding and removing a node.

Design doc"Shard a 10 TB user table with near-zero downtime — walk through key choice and rebalancing."

M04

Consistency & Transactions

Week 5 · 4 concepts

Goal. Stop using "eventually consistent" as an excuse; start using it as a decision with named consequences.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
CAP Theoremwhat C and A actually mean under partition — and how PACELC adds the latency trade-off people forget
Consistency Modelsstrong, sequential, causal, read-your-writes, eventual — and which ones users can actually notice
Eventual Consistencyanti-entropy, read repair, versioning and conflict resolution; what "eventually" costs in UX
Distributed Transactions2PC mechanics and why it blocks; Sagas with compensating actions; the outbox pattern and why dual writes corrupt data

Field noteConsistency is chosen per operation, not per system: strong for balance reads, inventory deduction, uniqueness; eventual for feeds, dashboards, search indexes; read-after-write as its own requirement (session pinning). And never forget: eventual consistency without a saga is data corruption with a delay.

LabImplement a saga with compensations for an order + payment two-service flow; then break it with a dual write and repair it with an outbox.

Design doc"Pick the consistency model for a shopping cart, a bank ledger, and a social feed. Defend each choice."

Part III — Performance & Resilience · Weeks 6–7

Control what the load does to you

Reads get fast with caching; systems stay up with resilience engineering. Two modules, one theme.

M05

Caching

Week 6 · 2 concepts

Goal. Make reads fast without making correctness subtly wrong. There are only two hard things in computer science, and this module is one of them.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Cachingthe layer stack (browser → CDN → app → DB), patterns (cache-aside, write-through, write-behind), eviction (LRU/LFU/TTL), and the thundering herd
Cache InvalidationTTL vs explicit purge vs versioned keys — and your story when data changes out-of-band

Field noteCache only when read:write > 10:1, staleness is acceptable, the computation is expensive, and projected hit rate > 50% — "cache per-user = cache with no hits = just a slow write." The four-layer stack compounds until ~99% of reads never touch disk, and cache hit ratio is the SLO at scale. A cache the system cannot survive without is not a cache — it is a tier of your architecture: defend the stampede path with single-flight, TTL jitter, and stale-while-revalidate.

LabAdd cache-aside to a real API, measure hit/miss ratios, then deliberately trigger a stampede and fix it with request coalescing or locks.

Design doc"Design the full cache stack for a product-detail page over 10M SKUs."

M06

Resilience

Week 7 · 2 concepts

Goal. Design for the dependency that will fail. It will. The only question is whether your system has an opinion about it.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Fault Tolerancetimeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, graceful degradation, chaos testing — and which retries make things worse (retry storms)
Idempotency & Data Latencyat-least-once vs exactly-once delivery, idempotency keys, deduplication windows, and why exactly-once is a contract you implement, not a property the network gives you

Field noteRetries need full jitter — sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)) — because fixed backoff syncs your retry batches into new spikes. Circuit breakers are a product decision: critical path fast-fails; non-critical paths fall back. Bulkheads: per-downstream pools contain failure. And: read-only retries are safe; state-changing retries without idempotency are bugs.

LabWrap a deliberately flaky dependency with a circuit breaker plus retry with exponential backoff and jitter; verify state stays consistent under duplicate deliveries.

Design doc"Make a payment API safe under duplicate submits and a 30-second provider outage."

Part IV — Architecture Patterns · Weeks 8–9

Interaction patterns at scale

Move work off the request path, get services to agree on who leads, and decide how many services you can actually afford.

M07

Async Systems & Coordination

Weeks 8–9 · 5 concepts

Goal. Learn the two moves that make distributed systems tractable: asynchronous work via queues, and coordination via discovery and leader election — then honestly price the microservices option.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
QueuesKafka vs RabbitMQ mental models, delivery semantics, retries, dead-letter queues, backpressure
Microservicesservice boundaries, data ownership, deployment independence — and the coupling that just moves into the network
Microservices vs Monolithsthe splitting costs, the modular-monolith-first option, and the Conway's-law argument
Service Discoveryregistries, health checks, client-side vs server-side discovery, and what happens during a partial partition
Leader Electionleases, consensus intuition (Raft), split-brain prevention — who leads when the leader is half-dead

Field noteKafka's durability is a per-topic dial, not a global setting: acks=0 for telemetry, acks=1 for user events, acks=all for billing — the configuration is the design. Team topology comes first: microservices below ~25 engineers is almost always a mistake; the database is the boundary — no DB extraction means a distributed monolith. Synchronous chains are how distributed systems get slow; asynchronous chains are how they stay debuggable.

LabBuild a mini message queue with at-least-once delivery, consumer retries, and an idempotent consumer. Redeliver on purpose; nothing may be lost or double-applied.

Design doc"Monolith or microservices for a 12-engineer startup shipping a marketplace? Write the decision memo both ways."

M09

Design Workshop & Capstone

Week 12 · capstone + mock interviews

Goal. Perform under pressure, and ship one thing you built end-to-end. The round is not a knowledge test — it is a judgment test, and the runbook below is the judgment, time-boxed. Memorize the shape; fill it with anything.

ClockPhaseWhat you doThe losing move
0:00–0:05ScopeThe 90-second opening, four moves: reframe ("Before I draw anything, I want to make sure I'm solving the right problem") → exactly three questions — scale, axis (read-heavy / write-heavy / latency-critical / durability-critical), scope ("what's explicitly out of scope?" — the magic one) → name your sequence → invite redirection. Write scope in a corner and refer back to it at least three times.Picking up the marker at second 12
0:05–0:10EstimateDerive four numbers aloud, in order: DAU → QPS → storage → bandwidth. "Assuming X, and assuming Y, that puts us around Z." State the peak multiplier. The arithmetic is the point, not the answer.Declaring numbers with no chain underneath
0:10–0:25The spineOne clean request→response path — the simplest thing that works. Then name the first bottleneck before going deep: "Given [number], the first thing that breaks is [X], because [fundamental limit]."Adding cache, queue, and six services before anything demands them
0:25–0:40Deep divePick by three filters: where senior trade-offs live, where your capacity numbers bite, where the interviewer's axis points. Seven steps: why this component → internals → alternatives → happy path → three failure modes → operations at 3 a.m. → "deeper, or move on?"Going deep on a commodity component
0:40–0:45Name what breaks + defendVolunteer the weakness first: "The bottleneck here is X. At 10× scale, Y breaks first. If I had more time I'd shard Z." Answer every "why this?" with the four-part sentence below.Presenting the design as failure-free

The four-part trade-off sentence — the move that wins the deep phase: "[Choice] because [reason], not [alternative] because [its cost], accepting [this choice's cost]." Example: "Kafka, because we need replay at this volume. If it were low-volume with complex routing, I'd use RabbitMQ. The replay requirement is what decides it."

The scale jump (around minute 32 — a reset, not a continuation): never "add more servers." Name the qualitative shifts — geographic distribution (a 50–150 ms cross-region floor), multi-layer caching (each layer its own invalidation story), async everywhere, distributed data — and the staff-level fifth: at 1× the bottleneck is compute; at 100× the bottleneck is coordination.

Pushback protocol: "You're right to push on that — let me redo it" is a reset, not a defeat. Pivot, don't restart: keep the board, fix the pushed piece. Defend only about 1 in 10 pushbacks, anchored to a specific constraint or number. The interviewer is grading adaptability, not the revised architecture.

Self-audit before every mock — the eight fatal mistakes: designing before scoping · demonstrating knowledge instead of judgment · covering everything at shallow depth · skipping capacity math · single points of failure · no trade-off talk · ignoring security · no observability story.

DrillsInterviews Vol IV (ten chapters = ten drills) · Interview Cheatsheet (framework, mistakes, four worked patterns: feed fan-out hybrid, URL shortener, chat, search) · Survival Kit (the opening + round checklist) · 45-Minute Map · Visual Atlas §01 (the interview, drawn).

CapstoneChoose one, via Build Your Own X: a Raft-based KV store with leader election · a message queue with delivery guarantees · a load balancer · a rate limiter · a URL shortener with a full design doc and cache/CDN layering. Defend every major capstone decision with the four-part sentence.

PrepWork through Tech Interview Handbook and Coding Interview University in parallel from Week 8 — not Week 12.

Part VI — The Field Manual · Weeks 13–15

Judgment under pressure

The textbooks stop where the job gets hard. Two modules from the field library: run what you design, then make your judgment visible.

M10

Production Operations

Weeks 13–14 · incident command · code review · written record

Goal. Run what you design — incidents, reviews, and the written record. The first ten minutes of an incident decide whether it takes ten minutes or three hours.

A · Incident command. One rule: read before you write — every diagnostic step is read-only until the failure is classified. Work the order: Classify → Confirm → Narrow → Mitigate → Verify. Three search-space questions: What changed? (most incidents are something that changed in the last hour) · How wide? (endpoint / service / region / everything) · Is it accelerating? (accelerating means mitigate now, diagnose later). Confirm with numbers, not vibes — check the dependency's own metrics, not your latency to it. Mitigate with reversible moves, one change at a time. And the capacity trap: adding instances makes retry storms, stampedes, and leaks worse.

Failure shapeSignatureFirst check
Connection pool exhaustionEverything slow, DB idlePool utilization / acquire-wait — not query time
Retry stormLoad far above real traffic, no deploy behind itCaller retry / backoff config
Cache stampedeDB fine, then dead instantly at peakA shared expiry timestamp
Resource leakSlow climb for hours or days, then a wallThe trend, not one snapshot
Swallowed exceptionLogs say success, users see failureCatch blocks near the failure
Deadlock / lock contentionEveryone waiting, no one workingLock order, not lock count
"Everything is green"Dashboards fine, users blockedOne real user path, end to end

B · Case study — the 02:43 outage. Payments P99 climbs 280 ms → 4.2 s; the pool saturates; every DB dashboard looks healthy. Thirty-five minutes go missing in the wrong layer — until the senior's one question: "What are the ten connections actually doing right now?" Answer: 8 of 10 connections idle in transaction, wait_event_type = Client — the app, not the DB, holds them. Root cause: a synchronous fraud-API HTTP call inside @Transactional — ~55 ms of DB work holding each connection ~8 s while the third party degraded: a 31× throughput collapse. The fix is three structural lines (move the call before the transaction); the hardening is an audit of all 16 transactional methods, a circuit breaker, and a 30 s → 10 s acquisition timeout. The rule: transaction duration equals the duration of the slowest synchronous thing inside it. The meta-lesson: the same shape was diagnosed in 4h12m, then 1h47m, 38m, 11m, 7m, 4m, 2m, and finally 90 seconds — pattern recognition is exposure, compressed.

C · Code review as an incident filter. The senior question: "What would have to be true for this to break in production six months from now?" Twenty patterns across eight families — correctness slips, concurrency races, database anti-patterns (N+1, missing indexes without CONCURRENTLY, transactions held across HTTP), API contract drift, reliability omissions (clients without timeouts, retries without idempotency keys, sync calls on the critical path), security slips (log injection, IDOR / tenant scoping), test theater, and the reviewer's own judgment. Reviewer discipline: 0–3 load-bearing comments per PR; block for data loss, security, breaking APIs, migration order; approve-with-concerns for the rest. Bring data, not taste.

D · Writing it down. The ADR, six sections — decision-statement title, status, context (≤2 paragraphs), decision, alternatives considered (the section lazy authors cut and future readers most need), consequences — readable in five minutes, in version control next to the code. The postmortem: blameless means actions, not actors; max 3 action items, each with a named owner and date; review the corpus quarterly and invest in the recurring category.

LabsRun the 7-shape triage against the 02:43 timeline and write its postmortem · review a seeded PR and leave exactly three load-bearing comments · write the ADR for your M03 sharding decision.

M11

The Senior Track

Week 15 · decisions · communication · leveling & offers

Goal. Judgment, made visible. Senior engineering is not a knowledge problem — it is a judgment problem, and this module makes the judgment inspectable.

A · The decision framework. Before any architecture decision, four questions: Is this reversible? What is the blast radius? What does it cost to not decide? What does it constrain downstream? Two-way doors vs one-way doors; the asymmetry rule — irreversible + wide blast radius deserves ~10× the deliberation. Five cost dimensions, of which infrastructure (the only one on the slide) is rarely the largest: engineering time, operational tax ("who is on call for this in three years?"), cognitive load, opportunity cost. Most bad architecture decisions are not made deliberately. They are made by default.

DecisionThe rule that settles itThe loss case
Kafka vs RabbitMQThroughput / replay / ordering / ops — under ~5K msg/s the choice doesn't differentiate; replay needs a log500 emails/min on a 3-broker Kafka cluster: ~10× the cost of RabbitMQ
PostgreSQL vs DynamoDBAccess-pattern predictability decides; Postgres is the default until proven otherwise; after 3–4 GSIs "you are operating five tables"200 req/s SaaS "for web scale": 5 GSIs by month 9, a 4-month migration back
Monolith vs microservicesTeam topology first (Conway); below ~25 engineers ≈ almost always a mistake; the modular monolith keeps the door open12 engineers, 12 services: every feature touched 3+, 8 merged back, ~2 engineer-years lost
Consistency per operationStrong for money, inventory, uniqueness; eventual for feeds and dashboards; read-after-write is its own requirementEventually-consistent inventory cache oversold 4,000 units in a flash sale
REST / gRPC / GraphQLBy consumer profile; most systems need one style, a few need two, almost nothing needs threeThree API styles over one data model: drift, tripled on-call, 2 of 3 deprecated
Sync vs asyncLatency budget + failure cost + idempotency decide; async-by-default for anything the user doesn't wait onPayment auth made async: silent failures, 2 months of reconciliation infra
Replicas vs sharding>80% reads → replicas (reversible); >30% writes → sharding (effectively irreversible); analyze hot keys firstSharded at 8K writes/s, 95% reads: ~1 engineer-year round trip for no benefit
Build vs buyCore → build; context → buy; critical-but-not-core → deliberate, time-boxed, with documented exit criteriaFeature flags built because "the vendor was too expensive": ~1 engineer-year wasted

B · Communication — the radius of thinking. What committees measure is how far the consequences of your decisions traveled: team → org → company (the scope ladder). The highest-leverage sentence: "We considered [X], and chose not to, because [constraint] mattered more than [what X would have given us]." Impact formula: reach × severity × business value — and if you lack the number, show the estimation method instead of inventing one. Phrase swaps: "I built X" → "I chose X over Y, because Z mattered more"; "It went well" → "It hit [metric] against a baseline of [metric]"; "I escalated it" → a documented last resort, taken with data. Self-audit against the eight failure patterns: too technical · no system thinking · escalated as first move · can't quantify · process without outcome · no trade-offs named · no mentorship signal · no story about being wrong.

C · The offer. Level is the biggest lever — negotiate the level before the number. Scope and ownership over title. The real money moves in equity and sign-on, not base; competing offers in writing are the whole game. Never accept on the call — the relief is the expensive emotion. Compensation signals, weak vs strong: writes the most code → reduces the need for code; says yes to everything → says no, with a reason; knows the most tools → knows which tool not to use.
