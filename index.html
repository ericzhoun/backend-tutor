How this course works

Every week runs the same four-step rhythm. The order matters: you diagram before you build, and you write a design doc after you build — because the doc is where the trade-offs stop being hand-wavy.

StepTimeWhat you do
Read3 hStudy the module's concepts from the core reading libraries.
Diagram2 hRedraw the architecture from memory — no peeking. If you can't draw it, you can't build it.
Build3 hImplement the module's lab: small, working, real. A queue that "mostly works" teaches you exactly-once lies.
Design doc1 hOne page: requirements, estimates, architecture, top two bottlenecks. The prompt is given per module.

Two standing rules. Case studies come after concepts, never before — they are the exam, not the lecture. And every module ends by answering the same question in writing: what breaks at 100× scale?

From Week 13 the rhythm is unchanged, but the material switches from textbooks to the field document library (section 07): labs become drills, reviews, and written records — the same skills, tested where they were learned. Every module ends with a quiz: six questions (five for the lab modules), instant grading, and an explanation for every answer.

Module syllabus

Part I — Foundations · Weeks 1–2

Know what you're up against

Before architecture: the cost math of scale, then everything between a user's tap and your server.

M01

Scaling Fundamentals

Week 1 · 2 concepts · drill-heavy

Goal. Reason about load mathematically before touching any architecture diagram. Most design failures are arithmetic failures wearing an architecture costume.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Horizontal vs Vertical Scalingwhen each is cheaper, their failure and cost curves, and why "just add machines" hides state problems rather than solving them
Back-of-the-Envelope EstimationQPS, storage, and bandwidth math from first principles; the latency numbers every engineer should know cold

DrillMemorize the anchor numbers: 86,400 s/day; RTT ~1 ms same-region, 50–150 ms cross-region; SSD sequential ~100 MB/s and random reads 50–100 µs; a single Postgres primary handles ~10–50K writes/s; a single Redis ~100K ops/s. Practice the capacity chain aloud: DAU → QPS → storage → bandwidth — "Assuming X, and assuming Y, that puts us around Z." Plan for peak, not average: single-region consumer 2.5–3×, global 1.5–2×, B2B business-hours 4–5×, flash-sale 10×+.

LabEstimate QPS, storage growth per day, bandwidth, and cache footprint for five products: URL shortener, news feed, group chat, video streaming, ride hailing. Worked reference: 1B URLs/day ≈ 11.6K writes/s; at a 100:1 read ratio, ≈ 1.16M reads/s.

Field readingSystem Design Reality (the 1K → 10M phase narrative) · Interviews Vol IV ch. 02 (capacity without guessing).

Design doc"Estimate the infrastructure for a WhatsApp-class messenger at 50M DAU."

Lesson

Scaling starts as arithmetic, not architecture. The chain is always the same: users → actions per user → operations per day → average QPS → peak QPS → storage per day → bandwidth. Write the chain down before drawing a single box, and say your assumptions out loud: "Assuming X, and assuming Y, that puts us around Z." A derived wrong number beats a guessed right one — the chain is what you'll use to spend the company's real money.

Worked example — a URL shortener at 1B links/day: writes = 1e9 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11.6K/s; at a 100:1 read ratio, reads ≈ 1.16M/s; storage at ~500 B/row ≈ 500 GB/day, roughly a petabyte over five years; with 20% hot keys and a 1-hour TTL, cache ≈ 400 GB. Each number unlocks a decision: consider sharding past ~50K writes/s, tier storage past ~10 TB, CDN past ~100 MB/s egress.

Anchor numberValue to memorize
Seconds per day86,400 ≈ 100K — divide daily totals by 100K for a quick average
Round-trip time~1 ms same-region · 50–150 ms cross-region
SSDSequential ~100 MB/s class · random reads 50–100 µs
Single Postgres primary~10–50K writes/s sustained — the practical sharding threshold
Single Redis~100K ops/s — the hot-key ceiling

Vertical vs horizontal. Vertical scaling is underrated: one big machine — one OS to patch, one profiler to read — covers most services' real needs. One field case replaced three layers of "scalable" infrastructure with a single 64-core box plus DB tuning and cut p99 by 40%. Horizontal earns its complexity only for embarrassingly parallel, stateless work where failure isolation matters. Its hidden taxes: in-process caches go cold on every scale-out, sticky sessions fight the load balancer, background workers fight over queues, and each instance multiplies your downstream connection footprint. State is the real reason "just add machines" fails: stateless request handling scales by cloning; state does not.

Know your phase. Every architecture decision has a scale at which it becomes wrong — and both failure modes are real: premature optimization (designing for 10M with 1K users) and never optimizing until something breaks catastrophically. The phases: 1–10K users (one box, vertical) · 10–100K (read replicas, CDN, a load balancer) · 100K–1M (cache, connection pooling, extract hot paths, async) · 1M+ (sharding, multi-region, event-driven). And the redesign signal: if you've doubled the database tier three times and p99 hasn't moved, the answer is the query — an O(n²) scan scales with data, not with hardware.

Quiz · M01 — 5 questions

1. A product has 100M DAU and each user makes 2 requests/day. Peak traffic is 3× average. Peak QPS?

Average = 200M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 2,315 QPS; peak = 2,315 × 3 ≈ 6,944. Production systems fail at peak, not at average — state the multiplier out loud.

2. The sustained write throughput of a single PostgreSQL primary is roughly…

~10–50K writes/s sustained is the working ceiling — it's why the sharding conversation starts seriously past that range, and why Redis' ~100K ops/s is the hot-key ceiling next to it.

3. Which of these is effectively a one-way door (irreversible once real data flows)?

The shard key decides every future query's shape and requires re-sharding to undo — treat it as irreversible and give it ~10× the deliberation. The other three are reversible within days.

4. Horizontal scaling is most likely to disappoint when…

Scale-out works by cloning stateless request handling. In-process caches go cold on each new instance, sticky sessions break even distribution, and each instance multiplies downstream connection footprints — the load spreads, the benefit doesn't.

5. You've doubled the database tier three times and p99 latency is unchanged. The most likely next move:

Scaling repeatedly without proportional improvement means the bottleneck scales with data, not hardware — a query-shaped problem. Hardware hides it; only the redesign removes it.
M02

The Request Path

Week 2 · 4 concepts

Goal. Master everything between a user's tap and your server — and know exactly where each concern lives: edge, gateway, or service.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Load BalancingL4 vs L7, algorithms (round robin, least connections, consistent hashing), health checks, and removing the balancer itself as a single point of failure
CDNedge caching, pull vs push, cache-control headers, and what a CDN cannot help with (dynamic, personalized, low-fan-out content)
API Gatewayrouting, auth termination, aggregation — and when the gateway becomes a bottleneck or a monolith-in-waiting
Rate Limitingtoken bucket vs sliding window, distributed limiters, and the fail-open vs fail-closed decision

LabImplement a token-bucket rate limiter; then draw the complete request path for a read-heavy product, edge to database.

Design doc"Launch-day traffic is 40× forecast. Where does the path break first, and in what order?"

Lesson

The path, in order: client → DNS → CDN edge → load balancer (L4/L7) → API gateway → service → data. TLS typically terminates at the edge or the balancer. Every concern has a home — the design review question is always "which layer owns this?"

Load balancing. L4 balancers are fast and connection-level; L7 balancers read the request — path, host, headers — and route smartly. Algorithms: round-robin ignores actual load; least-connections adapts to slow consumers; consistent hashing gives session affinity without a session store. Health checks evict dead nodes — that's what makes the balancer itself not a single point of failure.

CDN. Pull mode (edge fetches on miss) protects the origin; push mode pre-warms. Cache-control headers and versioned URLs make invalidation instant. A CDN cannot fix dynamic, personalized, low-fan-out responses — the per-user tail still lands on your origin.

API gateway. Routing, auth termination, aggregation, rate limiting, metrics — all cross-cutting, all belong at the edge. The smell to watch: business logic creeping into the gateway. Now every product change deploys through a shared component owned by nobody — a monolith-in-waiting.

Rate limiting. Token bucket (capacity + refill rate) allows controlled bursts; sliding window is strict over the window. Enforce atomically — Redis + Lua — or two instances will double-count. Tier it: global → per API key → per endpoint → per user, and return X-RateLimit-* + Retry-After so well-behaved clients can back off. On limiter-store failure, the production default for a public read-heavy API is fail open with an alert; money paths fail closed.

Two adjacent traps. Pagination: OFFSET at page 500 reads 10,020 rows and discards 10,000 — cursor pagination on a (created_at, id) tuple reads 20 rows at any depth. Real-time: long polling works anywhere and wastes; SSE is one-way over HTTP/2 with auto-reconnect; WebSockets are bidirectional but stateful — cross-server fan-out needs Redis pub/sub or sticky sessions.

Quiz · M02 — 5 questions

1. Which load-balancing algorithm gives session affinity without a session store?

Consistent hashing maps the same client/key to the same node, and rebalances minimally when nodes join or leave — affinity without sticky-session state.

2. A client needs to burst above its average rate occasionally. Which limiter fits?

A token bucket accumulates unused capacity up to a cap, so short bursts pass while the sustained rate stays bounded. Sliding windows enforce the rate strictly; fixed windows double-count across boundaries.

3. Your rate limiter's Redis dies. It fronts a public, read-heavy API. Production default?

For most public APIs, rejecting everyone because the limiter is down is worse than briefly trusting your clients — fail open, page someone. Money-moving paths are the exception: they fail closed.

4. Why does OFFSET pagination collapse at depth?

OFFSET walks and throws away every prior row — cost grows linearly with depth. A cursor on (created_at, id) seeks straight to the position: 20 rows at any depth.

5. Which of these is a design smell in an API gateway?

Cross-cutting concerns belong at the edge; product logic doesn't. Logic in the gateway means every feature deploys through a shared component — a monolith-in-waiting with the worst ownership in the company.

Part II — The Data Layer · Weeks 3–5

State is where systems get hard

Scale storage deliberately. Choose consistency like a contract, not an accident.

M03

Storage at Scale

Weeks 3–4 · 4 concepts · build week

Goal. Make databases scale on purpose. Every sharding war story starts with a shard key chosen casually on a Friday.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Database Scalingindexes, denormalization, read replicas, and SQL vs NoSQL selection by access pattern — not fashion
Replicationleader–follower, multi-leader, and leaderless topologies; sync vs async trade-offs; failover and split brain
Shardingshard-key choice, hotspots, the real cost of resharding at 2 a.m., and consistent hashing as the fix
Partitioningrange vs hash partitioning, skew, and rebalancing strategies

LabBuild a key-value store with consistent-hashing sharding (routed through Build Your Own X). It must survive adding and removing a node.

Design doc"Shard a 10 TB user table with near-zero downtime — walk through key choice and rebalancing."

Lesson

Choose by access pattern. PostgreSQL is the default until your workload proves otherwise: real ACID, joins, ad-hoc queries, JSONB, logical replication, partitioning to billions of rows. Purpose-built stores win when access patterns are fixed and writes are extreme — the win case: a mobile app with two known key patterns at 80K+ writes/s on DynamoDB, whose only operational work was watching the bill. The trap: unpredictable access on a key-value store means a new global secondary index per query — by the 3rd–4th GSI, "you are operating five tables," and each new query becomes a three-week project.

Replicas before sharding. When reads dominate (read:write above ~3:1) or analytics blocks writes, read replicas absorb the load with a fraction of the engineering cost. Lag is real (~50 ms typical) — reads are eventually consistent unless pinned; read-your-writes pins the session to the primary for a few seconds after a write. The boundary rule: replicas are reversible; they add zero write throughput — every replica receives every write.

Sharding is the last resort with a one-way door. Shard when a table passes ~500 GB–1 TB after optimization, or writes exceed the largest single primary. Hash sharding distributes evenly but kills cross-shard range queries; range sharding keeps ranges local but the last shard collects all new signups — a built-in hotspot. Hot keys: one tenant pulling 80K ops/s against a ~50K/s partition ceiling gets sub-partitioned into 4 × 20K/s, trading strict ordering for throughput. Before sharding, exhaust indexes, query optimization, partitioning, replicas, and pooling — because after sharding you inherit app-level cross-shard joins, distributed transactions, N× schema migrations with drift risk, a separate aggregation layer for analytics, and per-shard backups. The loss case: sharded "in preparation" at 8K writes/s with 95% reads — ~1 engineer-year round trip for no benefit. The win case: 95%-read platform, 3 replicas in 2 weeks, survived 5× growth.

Quiz · M03 — 5 questions

1. A 500 GB table serves 95% reads. Single primary is at 20% write capacity. First move?

>80% reads → replicas; >30% writes → sharding. Replicas are reversible and cheap; sharding at this profile is a one-way door with no benefit — the famous ~1 engineer-year round trip.

2. The structural flaw of range-based sharding (A–F / G–M / N–Z)?

Range sharding keeps related rows together but monotonic keys pile onto the newest range. Hash distributes evenly but destroys cross-shard range queries — pick your poison by access pattern.

3. Why don't read replicas increase write throughput?

Replication copies the primary's full write stream. Only sharding splits the write load across independent primaries — which is exactly why it's the harder, irreversible decision.

4. "Read-your-writes" after a user updates their profile is best delivered by…

Session pinning (or a client-side overlay of own writes) buys read-your-writes for one user while everyone else keeps reading cheap replicas — per-operation consistency instead of global strong.

5. One tenant pushes 80K ops/s against a ~50K/s per-partition ceiling. The fix:

The hot-key problem has exactly one structural fix: split the key's traffic across multiple partitions. Relaxed ordering per sub-key is the accepted cost.
M04

Consistency & Transactions

Week 5 · 4 concepts

Goal. Stop using "eventually consistent" as an excuse; start using it as a decision with named consequences.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
CAP Theoremwhat C and A actually mean under partition — and how PACELC adds the latency trade-off people forget
Consistency Modelsstrong, sequential, causal, read-your-writes, eventual — and which ones users can actually notice
Eventual Consistencyanti-entropy, read repair, versioning and conflict resolution; what "eventually" costs in UX
Distributed Transactions2PC mechanics and why it blocks; Sagas with compensating actions; the outbox pattern and why dual writes corrupt data

Field noteConsistency is chosen per operation, not per system: strong for balance reads, inventory deduction, uniqueness; eventual for feeds, dashboards, search indexes; read-after-write as its own requirement (session pinning). And never forget: eventual consistency without a saga is data corruption with a delay.

LabImplement a saga with compensations for an order + payment two-service flow; then break it with a dual write and repair it with an outbox.

Design doc"Pick the consistency model for a shopping cart, a bank ledger, and a social feed. Defend each choice."

Lesson

CAP, honestly. Network partitions are rare but nonzero — that's the theorem. The trade you make every day is latency (the E in PACELC): a strong cross-region write costs 80–200 ms every single time, partition or not. Choose per operation, not per system.

The ladder: strong → sequential → causal → read-your-writes → eventual. Users notice exactly two failures: invariant violations (oversold inventory, negative balance) and their own writes vanishing. They rarely notice a 200 ms-stale feed. So: always strong for money-balance reads, inventory decrement under contention, uniqueness constraints, and permission checks; fine eventual for dashboards, feeds, counters, analytics, caches, and search indexes. The wallet runs on three profiles simultaneously: balance on the transaction path = strong (SELECT FOR UPDATE); own dashboard balance = read-your-writes (session pin); transaction history and view counts = eventual with bounded lag.

Distributed transactions. Two-phase commit blocks: locks are held while every coordinator answers, so a slow participant freezes everyone. A saga chains local transactions with compensating actions — debit wallet → credit merchant → append audit; if the credit fails, re-credit the wallet and append a reversal entry. Intermediate states are visible by design; the audit log is the reconciliation source of truth. The compensating action is the part everyone skips — designing it is the design. And the dual-write trap: writing to the database and the broker separately will diverge when the process dies between the two writes. The outbox pattern writes state + event in one local transaction and lets a relay publish.

Memorize the sentence: eventual consistency without a saga is data corruption with a delay. Under partition, "eventual" lag is measured in minutes; while degraded, hours. The loss case: an eventually-consistent inventory cache (≤2 s lag) oversold 4,000 units in a flash sale — display may lie; the deduction path may not.

Quiz · M04 — 5 questions

1. Which requirement must be strongly consistent?

Uniqueness is an invariant — two accounts with one username is corruption, not staleness. Counters, feeds, and dashboards tolerate bounded staleness by design.

2. Two-phase commit's practical cost:

2PC's blocking window is why teams reach for sagas: local transactions keep locks short and failures local, at the price of visible intermediate states.

3. The part of a saga that teams most often skip — and shouldn't:

"Debit wallet, credit merchant" is easy — the design is what happens when the credit fails. If you can't name the reversal, you don't have a saga; you have a distributed transaction with extra steps.

4. Writing the order to the database and publishing the event to Kafka as two separate writes will eventually…

Dual writes have no atomicity. The outbox writes state + event in one local transaction; a relay publishes. Kafka's durability settings don't help — the divergence is on your side of the network.

5. "Users must see their own post instantly; everyone else within a second." The right design:

Read-your-writes is its own rung on the ladder — cheap to deliver with a session pin, while the other 99.99% of reads stay on lagging replicas.

Part III — Performance & Resilience · Weeks 6–7

Control what the load does to you

Reads get fast with caching; systems stay up with resilience engineering. Two modules, one theme.

M05

Caching

Week 6 · 2 concepts

Goal. Make reads fast without making correctness subtly wrong. There are only two hard things in computer science, and this module is one of them.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Cachingthe layer stack (browser → CDN → app → DB), patterns (cache-aside, write-through, write-behind), eviction (LRU/LFU/TTL), and the thundering herd
Cache InvalidationTTL vs explicit purge vs versioned keys — and your story when data changes out-of-band

Field noteCache only when read:write > 10:1, staleness is acceptable, the computation is expensive, and projected hit rate > 50% — "cache per-user = cache with no hits = just a slow write." The four-layer stack compounds until ~99% of reads never touch disk, and cache hit ratio is the SLO at scale. A cache the system cannot survive without is not a cache — it is a tier of your architecture: defend the stampede path with single-flight, TTL jitter, and stale-while-revalidate.

LabAdd cache-aside to a real API, measure hit/miss ratios, then deliberately trigger a stampede and fix it with request coalescing or locks.

Design doc"Design the full cache stack for a product-detail page over 10M SKUs."

Lesson

The stack compounds. A CDN absorbs ~70% of traffic; a regional cache takes ~60% of what remains (→ ~88% cumulative); an in-process cache ~50% of the rest (→ ~94%); the database buffer pool ~80% of what's left (→ ~99%). Each layer's miss costs roughly 10× the layer above to serve — only ~1% of reads ever touch disk. That's why cache hit ratio is the SLO at scale, not a performance optimization on top of one.

Before caching, pass all four checks — read:write above ~10:1 · short staleness acceptable · the computation or query is genuinely expensive · projected hit rate above ~50%. Per-user data fails the test: "cache per-user = cache with no hits = just a slow write." And don't cache when the bottleneck is elsewhere — CPU, network, auth. Caching is a fourth-priority fix: profile the query, then indexes, then the read pattern, then Redis. Caches turn one bug into two bugs.

Patterns. Cache-aside: on miss, load from DB, set with TTL — simplest, default, stale until eviction; the miss path (~6 ms) costs ~30× the hit path (~200 µs). Write-through: every write updates cache and DB together — consistent, but write latency rises and the cache becomes load-bearing. Write-behind: fast writes with a loss window. Whatever you pick, name the invalidation strategy on day one — TTL (guaranteed staleness within the window), event-driven purge (fast but racy), or both. The real-world failure isn't the mechanism; it's never writing the staleness tolerance down — the split-brain user-email incident happened because nobody did.

Stampede. A hot key expires and 200 requests miss simultaneously → 200 identical queries → pool exhaustion. Defenses, each ~10 lines of code: single-flight / request coalescing (one in-flight load per key; 199 requests wait), TTL jitter (spread expiries), stale-while-revalidate (serve stale, refresh async), probabilistic early refresh. The incident that proves the stakes: Redis failover → 10-minute cold cache → 100% of reads hit the DB → DB down → cache stays empty because the DB is down. The rule: a cache the system cannot survive without is not a cache — it is a tier of your architecture. Treat it like one: monitor it, protect it, pre-warm it.

Quiz · M05 — 5 questions

1. The cache-when checklist requires…

All four — miss any one and the cache adds latency and a second failure mode without paying for itself. "Caches turn one bug into two bugs."

2. A hot key expires at peak; 200 requests miss simultaneously. First-line fix?

Coalescing collapses 200 identical queries into 1. A longer TTL just moves the cliff; more app servers multiply the herd. Full defense: coalescing + TTL jitter + stale-while-revalidate.

3. In cache-aside, the miss path vs the hit path costs roughly…

Hit ≈ 200 µs (Redis ~100 µs); miss = SELECT + SET with a round trip through the DB. That ratio is why hit ratio — not average latency — is the number to watch.

4. Why is "cache each user's profile page" usually an anti-pattern?

Caching pays when many readers share an entry. Per-user data fails the hit-rate and staleness checks — spend the effort on the shared layers instead.

5. After Redis failed over, the cold cache sent 100% of reads to the DB and took it down. Deepest lesson?

"A cache the system cannot survive without is not a cache — it is a tier of your architecture." This one had drifted from reversible optimization to unmonitored dependency.
M06

Resilience

Week 7 · 2 concepts

Goal. Design for the dependency that will fail. It will. The only question is whether your system has an opinion about it.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
Fault Tolerancetimeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, graceful degradation, chaos testing — and which retries make things worse (retry storms)
Idempotency & Data Latencyat-least-once vs exactly-once delivery, idempotency keys, deduplication windows, and why exactly-once is a contract you implement, not a property the network gives you

Field noteRetries need full jitter — sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)) — because fixed backoff syncs your retry batches into new spikes. Circuit breakers are a product decision: critical path fast-fails; non-critical paths fall back. Bulkheads: per-downstream pools contain failure. And: read-only retries are safe; state-changing retries without idempotency are bugs.

LabWrap a deliberately flaky dependency with a circuit breaker plus retry with exponential backoff and jitter; verify state stays consistent under duplicate deliveries.

Design doc"Make a payment API safe under duplicate submits and a 30-second provider outage."

Lesson

Timeouts are a budget. If the user-facing budget is 3 s, no downstream may hold a 5 s timeout — a downstream timeout longer than its upstream's remaining budget is functionally infinite. Keep upstream > downstream at every hop, and propagate deadlines (gRPC natively; context propagation elsewhere). Ask "what happens if this is slow?" not "what happens if it fails" — a slow dependency saturates thread and connection pools and cascades; a fast-failing one just errors.

Retries: exponential backoff with full jitter — sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)). Fixed backoff aligns everyone's retries into new spikes at +1s/+2s/+4s; jitter spreads them under capacity. Retries are load multipliers: cap attempts, keep a retry budget (~≤10% of traffic), and never retry a state-changing operation without an idempotency key — read-only retries are safe; state-changing retries without idempotency are bugs.

The circuit breaker: CLOSED → errors above ~50% over a 20-request window → OPEN, return the fallback in <1 ms → after ~30 s, HALF-OPEN, probe one request at a time: success closes, failure reopens. Pair every breaker with a real fallback. It's a product decision per dependency: critical path (payments, identity) fast-fails; non-critical (recommendations) degrades to a sensible default.

Bulkheads: one shared 100-connection pool means a hung paymentsvc bleeds the entire app. Per-downstream pools (PAY 50, PROF 8) contain the blast radius. Idempotency: every state-changing retry carries an idempotency key per logical attempt (the Stripe-Idempotency-Key pattern), checked before processing, conditional writes, key TTL 7–30 days. Exactly-once is a contract you implement with dedup + transactional processing — not a property the network hands you. And the operational layer: 3-2-1 backups (3 copies, 2 media, 1 offsite) with a monthly restore drill — the restore time you measure is your real RTO; RPO is the data you can afford to lose.

Quiz · M06 — 5 questions

1. Why "full jitter" on retry backoff?

Fixed backoff syncs every client's retries to the same +1s/+2s/+4s marks — new spikes. sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)) spreads them flat under capacity.

2. Your service allows 3 s end-to-end. A downstream sets a 5 s timeout. That timeout is…

Timeouts are budgets, not suggestions: downstream must be strictly shorter than the upstream's remaining budget, or the upstream's pool drains serving clients who are gone.

3. In HALF-OPEN state, a circuit breaker…

The probe sequence lets a recovering dependency prove itself without exposing the full load: one success closes; one failure re-opens for another cooldown.

4. A payment charge is retried after a lost response, with no idempotency key. The risk:

At-least-once delivery means redelivery is normal. State-changing operations need an idempotency key per logical attempt so the second attempt is recognized as a replay, not a new charge.

5. A bulkhead protects the system by…

POOL.PAY 50, POOL.PROF 8: when the profile service hangs, it exhausts only its own 8 slots. One shared pool lets a single slow dependency take the whole app down.

Part IV — Architecture Patterns · Weeks 8–9

Interaction patterns at scale

Move work off the request path, get services to agree on who leads, and decide how many services you can actually afford.

M07

Async Systems & Coordination

Weeks 8–9 · 5 concepts

Goal. Learn the two moves that make distributed systems tractable: asynchronous work via queues, and coordination via discovery and leader election — then honestly price the microservices option.

ConceptYou should be able to explain…
QueuesKafka vs RabbitMQ mental models, delivery semantics, retries, dead-letter queues, backpressure
Microservicesservice boundaries, data ownership, deployment independence — and the coupling that just moves into the network
Microservices vs Monolithsthe splitting costs, the modular-monolith-first option, and the Conway's-law argument
Service Discoveryregistries, health checks, client-side vs server-side discovery, and what happens during a partial partition
Leader Electionleases, consensus intuition (Raft), split-brain prevention — who leads when the leader is half-dead

LabBuild a mini message queue with at-least-once delivery, consumer retries, and an idempotent consumer. Redeliver on purpose; nothing may be lost or double-applied.

Design doc"Monolith or microservices for a 12-engineer startup shipping a marketplace? Write the decision memo both ways."

Lesson

A queue absorbs spikes, not permanent overload. Producers spike 200K/s against a DB that sustains 50K/s: depth grows during the spike and drains after — that's the win. Sustained overload just fills the disk. Alert when depth > workers × processing_time × 2, scale workers with depth, and watch the drain rate, not just the depth. Poison pills get bounded retries (3 × 5/10/15 s backoff), then a dead-letter queue carrying the original error — and a named owner watching it. The silent-ack cautionary tale: a catch block that acked failed messages "to avoid loops" quietly dropped ~9,000 messages over six months with every dashboard green. The rule that catches it: end-to-end reconciliation (received vs processed) as first-class observability, and no silent catch blocks in review.

Kafka vs RabbitMQ is log vs queue. Kafka: distributed commit log — offsets, native replay, per-partition ordering, consumer groups, a 3–5 broker cluster even with KRaft. RabbitMQ: a queue — ack deletes, competing consumers, single node is fine. Under ~5K msg/s the choice doesn't differentiate; replay needs a log; a low-throughput task queue doesn't want Kafka (the loss case: 500 emails/min on a 3-broker Kafka cluster — ~10× the cost of RabbitMQ, 6 engineer-months to learn that).

The durability dial is per topic: acks=0 (~1 ms, telemetry), acks=1 (~3–5 ms, user events), acks=all (~10–20 ms, billing/audit); RF=3 with min ISR=2 survives one broker failure. Partition by the key that needs ordering; hot partitions (the top 0.1% of tenants) get sub-key partitioning — trading strict ordering for throughput. The configuration is the design. Delivery: at-least-once is the default, so consumers must be idempotent (dedup table, upsert, conditional writes) and commit after processing; exactly-once is expensive and only cheap Kafka-to-Kafka.

Monolith vs microservices is an org decision (Conway's law: service topology follows team topology). 1–10 engineers → monolith; 10–25 → modular monolith or 2–4 services; 25–60 → microservices where boundaries match teams. Extract for a stable bounded context with independent scale, deploy, or ownership needs — "we might need to scale someday" is a hope, not a condition. The database is the boundary: a shared database means a distributed monolith — every cost of microservices, none of the independence.

Sync vs async: go async when the user doesn't wait on the result, the dependency eats more than half the latency budget, failure shouldn't propagate, or the third party's tail is variable — and make every async command idempotent. Synchronous chains are how distributed systems get slow; asynchronous chains are how they stay debuggable. Coordination closes the part: discovery = registry + health checks (client-side picks, server-side hides); leader election = leases with TTL and majority terms (Raft intuition); the half-dead leader is exactly why leases expire and fencing tokens exist.

Quiz · M07 — 5 questions

1. Kafka acks setting for billing/audit events?

The dial is per topic: 0 for telemetry (~1 ms), 1 for user events (~3–5 ms), all for anything you cannot afford to lose. "The configuration is the design."

2. A reasonable queue-depth alert fires when depth exceeds…

Depth beyond ~2 work-cycles means the spike has outrun the drain rate — that's the line between "absorbing a spike" and "filling the disk."

3. A message consistently crashes the consumer (a poison pill). Correct handling:

Acking silently is how 9,000 messages vanish over six months with green dashboards. Forever-retrying blocks the partition. Bounded retries + DLQ + reconciliation is the pattern.

4. A 12-engineer startup is building a marketplace. The default architecture:

Below ~25 engineers, microservices is almost always a mistake. The modular monolith — one deployment, strict module lines, in-process calls — keeps the extraction door open for when team topology actually demands it.

5. Kafka's ordering guarantee holds…

Order exists only within a partition. Global order requires a single partition, which kills parallelism — so the partition key choice is the ordering design.

Judgment under pressure

M10

Production Operations

Weeks 13–14 · incident command · code review · written record

Goal. Run what you design — incidents, reviews, and the written record. The first ten minutes of an incident decide whether it takes ten minutes or three hours.

A · Incident command. One rule: read before you write — every diagnostic step is read-only until the failure is classified. Work the order: Classify → Confirm → Narrow → Mitigate → Verify. Three search-space questions: What changed? (most incidents are something that changed in the last hour) · How wide? (endpoint / service / region / everything) · Is it accelerating? (accelerating means mitigate now, diagnose later). Confirm with numbers, not vibes — check the dependency's own metrics, not your latency to it. Mitigate with reversible moves, one change at a time. And the capacity trap: adding instances makes retry storms, stampedes, and leaks worse.

Failure shapeSignatureFirst check

B · Case study — the 02:43 outage. Payments P99 climbs 280 ms → 4.2 s; the pool saturates; every DB dashboard looks healthy. Thirty-five minutes go missing in the wrong layer — until the senior's one question: "What are the ten connections actually doing right now?" Answer: 8 of 10 connections idle in transaction, wait_event_type = Client — the app, not the DB, holds them. Root cause: a synchronous fraud-API HTTP call inside @Transactional — ~55 ms of DB work holding each connection ~8 s while the third party degraded: a 31× throughput collapse. The fix is three structural lines (move the call before the transaction); the hardening is an audit of all 16 transactional methods, a circuit breaker, and a 30 s → 10 s acquisition timeout. The rule: transaction duration equals the duration of the slowest synchronous thing inside it. The meta-lesson: the same shape was diagnosed in 4h12m, then 1h47m, 38m, 11m, 7m, 4m, 2m, and finally 90 seconds — pattern recognition is exposure, compressed.

C · Code review as an incident filter. The senior question: "What would have to be true for this to break in production six months from now?" Twenty patterns across eight families — correctness slips, concurrency races, database anti-patterns (N+1, missing indexes without CONCURRENTLY, transactions held across HTTP), API contract drift, reliability omissions (clients without timeouts, retries without idempotency keys, sync calls on the critical path), security slips (log injection, IDOR / tenant scoping), test theater, and the reviewer's own judgment. Reviewer discipline: 0–3 load-bearing comments per PR; block for data loss, security, breaking APIs, migration order; approve-with-concerns for the rest. Bring data, not taste.

D · Writing it down. The ADR, six sections — decision-statement title, status, context (≤2 paragraphs), decision, alternatives considered (the section lazy authors cut and future readers most need), consequences — readable in five minutes, in version control next to the code. The postmortem: blameless means actions, not actors; max 3 action items, each with a named owner and date; review the corpus quarterly and invest in the recurring category.

Lesson — the diagnostic technique

The reusable core of the 02:43 story is a two-query check. Query one: on pg_stat_activity, compare max_held_seconds vs max_query_seconds — when held ≫ query, connections are parked, not working: the pool-exhaustion signature. Query two: look at the longest-held connections' wait_event_type — Client means the database is waiting for the application to send work (app-side hold); IO or Lock means a genuine database problem. Then trace the last SQL back to the transaction's entry-point method and search it for the five culprits: synchronous external HTTP, synchronous messaging publish, file/S3 I/O, explicit sleeps, and remote-cache misses inside the transaction. Six months of incident archaeology compresses into those two queries — the first one you ask is the expertise.

Quiz · M10 — 6 questions

1. The first-ten-minutes investigation order:

Blast radius before cause; a live request before a dashboard; cut the search space in half per check; one reversible change at a time; verify against the original symptom.

2. Signature: every endpoint slow, the database dashboard idle. First check?

Everything slow + DB idle = requests queued on connections that are never returned. It's not a capacity problem — find what's holding the connections (usually an external call inside a transaction).

3. In the 02:43 outage, the pool was saturated because…

Transaction duration equals the duration of the slowest synchronous thing inside it. Raising the pool size would have bought ninety seconds — then the bigger pool would have exhausted the same way.

4. The third-party payment API starts returning 503s. First move?

"The first 90 seconds of a third-party outage decide whether the next two hours are calm or chaotic." Retries amplify load on both sides; degradation beats amplification.

5. Errors spiked 28 minutes after a clean deploy. The rollback default applies unless…

"The rollback is the safe answer. The hotfix is the heroic answer. Heroes ship two bugs." Rolling forward under pressure produces a second bug ~4 times in 10.

6. Postmortem action items:

Twelve action items guarantees none happen. Blameless means the timeline names actions, not actors — and the corpus gets reviewed quarterly for the recurring category.
M11

The Senior Track

Week 15 · decisions · communication · leveling & offers

Goal. Judgment, made visible. Senior engineering is not a knowledge problem — it is a judgment problem, and this module makes the judgment inspectable.

A · The decision framework. Before any architecture decision, four questions: Is this reversible? What is the blast radius? What does it cost to not decide? What does it constrain downstream? Two-way doors vs one-way doors; the asymmetry rule — irreversible + wide blast radius deserves ~10× the deliberation. Five cost dimensions, of which infrastructure (the only one on the slide) is rarely the largest: engineering time, operational tax ("who is on call for this in three years?"), cognitive load, opportunity cost. Most bad architecture decisions are not made deliberately. They are made by default.

B · Communication — the radius of thinking. What committees measure is how far the consequences of your decisions traveled: team → org → company (the scope ladder). The highest-leverage sentence: "We considered [X], and chose not to, because [constraint] mattered more than [what X would have given us]." The real money moves in equity and sign-on, not base; competing offers in writing are the whole game. Never accept on the call — the relief is the expensive emotion. Compensation signals, weak vs strong: writes the most code → reduces the need for code; says yes to everything → says no, with a reason; knows the most tools → knows which tool not to use.

Quiz · M11 — 6 questions

Reversibility first: it sets how much deliberation everything else deserves. Then blast radius, cost of not deciding, and second-order effects.

2. Which of these is a one-way door?

Event schemas are add-only once consumers exist; the multi-tenancy model, sharding key, and public API contracts live in the same category. Retry policy and TTLs are two-way doors.

"The relief is the expensive emotion." Level is the biggest lever — negotiate it before the number; the real money moves in equity and sign-on; competing offers in writing are the whole game.
